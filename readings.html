Reflection on “In Plato's Cave” from On Photography by Susan Sontag.

I love still images because they let me hold onto moments that would otherwise vanish. I can take 50 photos from one night out, and each one contains a different mood, a different micro story, or something small but unforgettable, like a cat caught at the perfect angle mid-lick. I like when a photo becomes a conversation without needing an explanation, because the framing alone makes someone curious.

After reading Sontag, I think more about how photographs get treated like pieces of reality even though they are always choices. A photo can feel like a window, like it is simply showing “what happened,” but it is still selective. Someone decided where to stand, what to include, what to crop out, and what moment counts as the moment. That is why images have so much influence. They do not just record life, they shape what we notice and what we believe is worth noticing. Sontag writes, “While a painting or a prose description can never be other than a narrowly selective interpretation, a photograph can be treated as a narrowly selective transparency.” [excerpt]

That makes photography feel like a responsibility, not just a hobby. Like most people, I hate unflattering photos, so when I photograph people, I try to capture them with dignity. But I do not chase perfection as much as honesty. Sometimes the candid feeling adds character and tells the truth better than perfect lighting or a symmetrical pose. I think the ethical part shows up in small decisions: not turning people into props, not flattening them into stereotypes, and not using a camera to take something from someone without care.

The part that hit me hardest is how photographs can be both comforting and painful. They freeze a moment, but they also remind you that the moment is gone. Sontag writes, “A photograph is both a pseudo‑presence and a token of absence. Like a wood fire in a room, photographs—especially those of people, of distant landscapes and faraway cities, of the vanished past.” I have experienced a lot of loss, so I cherish taking photos because it feels like saving something for the future. I never met my paternal grandparents, but seeing photographs of them living their lives, not posed, lets me feel close to them in a real way. The image becomes a kind of presence, while also making the absence undeniable, which is exactly why photos can carry so much emotion.

At the same time, I understand Sontag’s warning about how image culture can become compulsive, like reality needs to be confirmed by a photograph. I feel that tension constantly on social media, where images can move people emotionally while also stripping context and history away. I do not want my work to be shallow or numbing. If I were representing the world at this moment, I would focus on contrast: beauty next to exhaustion, connection next to isolation, performance next to private life, the polished image next to the truth behind it. Ultimately, I want my images to make people feel something real, not just react. My goal is for the lens to work like my eyes and the shutter to hold memory, not replace living.

With ‘AI slop’ distorting our reality, the world is sleepwalking into disaster by Nesrine Malik.

Nesrine Malik’s article made me rethink AI, not just as a tool but as a creator of a visual world. She argues that AI images are not only fake or low quality but so common that they change how we see reality. She describes two types of images online: real ones and low-effort AI ones. The problem is not only confusion between them. The larger issue is that AI images begin to shape how we read real images, until everything feels unstable and less emotional. Malik explains that AI images spread easily because they often arrive through trusted spaces like WhatsApp. When an image comes from friends or family, we tend to trust the sender more than the content. This shows that misinformation is not just a public problem. It becomes personal inside private chats where there are fewer checks and less questioning of what is real. She also argues that AI images are not neutral. They often present fantasy versions of race, gender, and power that lean toward right-wing or authoritarian ideas. This happens because AI models learn from existing data that already carries bias. These images do not appear by accident. They recycle and smooth over old hierarchies, presenting them as natural and attractive. Political fantasies start to feel normal, even when they are manipulative. What stayed with me most was her point about desensitization. Our feeds place everything side by side: war, memes, arrests, AI fantasies, propaganda, and comforting images. This constant mix makes everything feel flat. Real suffering appears next to silly or fake content, and we scroll past both with the same attention. The result is not just misinformation. It is exhaustion and confusion. Images lose their emotional power because they are always competing for our eyes. This connects directly to what we are learning in class about media and visual literacy. We talk about how framing and editing shape what people notice first in an image. Malik’s article shows what happens when that whole system loses order and trust. If every image is designed to grab attention for a second, then understanding how images work becomes a form of responsibility. When we create or share images, we are adding to a larger visual environment. We have to ask whether our work helps clarify reality or only adds more noise. I recognize this in my own social media use. My feed often jumps from serious news about violence or protest to lighthearted memes and AI-generated pictures. The emotional whiplash can feel overwhelming. Sometimes I catch myself scrolling past important stories because they look like everything else. Malik’s article makes me more aware of my role in this. It pushes me to be more careful about the images I share and to slow down before I accept something as real.
